# Agreement Is Cheap

Agreement is often mistaken for alignment.

In meetings, people nod. Stakeholders say yes. Vendors confirm understanding. Leaders appear supportive. The meeting ends with a sense of progress. But two weeks later, it becomes clear that everyone agreed to a different version of the same decision.

That is not alignment. That is implied consensus.

In advisory work, one of the most important responsibilities is to separate polite agreement from executable commitment. A group can agree conceptually while still being misaligned on scope, priority, funding, ownership, timelines, operating impact, or success measures.

This is especially common in transformation programs where multiple teams see the same initiative through different lenses. Business leaders focus on outcomes. Technology teams focus on architecture and integration. Operations teams focus on process impact. Finance focuses on cost control. Security focuses on risk. Training focuses on adoption. Each group may agree with the direction, but not necessarily with the delivery implications.

A useful consulting frame is:

> Assumptions, ownership, constraints, and measures.

Before treating agreement as real alignment, ask four questions:

1. What are we assuming?
2. Who owns the next step?
3. What could block execution?
4. How will we know this worked?

This connects well to Lean Six Sigma thinking. In Lean Six Sigma, problems are not solved through opinion alone. They are clarified through definitions, measures, root causes, controls, and evidence. If the team cannot define the process, the defect, the owner, and the metric, then agreement is not yet operational.

For example, a leadership team may agree to “improve reporting.” That phrase sounds clear, but it can mean different things. One stakeholder may mean executive dashboards. Another may mean operational queue reporting. Another may mean data quality. Another may mean KPI governance. Another may mean automated insights.

Without clarification, the team will move quickly in different directions.

A stronger advisory statement would be:

> “Before we close, let’s confirm what we are assuming, who owns the next step, what risk could block us, and what decision we are making today.”

This approach does not slow down delivery. It prevents false starts.

TOGAF also reinforces this principle through architecture governance. Strategic alignment is not just agreement at a high level. It requires traceability from business objectives to capabilities, processes, data, applications, and technology. If that traceability is missing, alignment is fragile.

Agreement is easy because it asks very little of people. Commitment is harder because it requires clarity, ownership, and trade-offs.

**Advisor takeaway:** Agreement closes the conversation. Alignment clarifies the path. Commitment creates business value.
