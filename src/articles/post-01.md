# If Everybody Owns It, Nobody Owns It

In business transformation, unclear ownership is one of the quietest ways value disappears.

Organizations rarely fail because nobody cares. More often, they fail because too many people care in an unstructured way. Everyone contributes, everyone has an opinion, everyone attends the meeting, but nobody is clearly accountable for moving the work from discussion to execution.

This is where complexity becomes dangerous. A transformation program may include business stakeholders, technology teams, operations, finance, vendors, security, data teams, training leads, and executive sponsors. On paper, that looks collaborative. In practice, it can become a governance gap if accountability is not explicit.

A common phrase in meetings is, “The team will take care of it.” That sounds safe, but it is often too vague to be executable. Which team? Which person? By when? With what authority? Against which outcome?

From a consulting perspective, this is where RACI becomes useful. A RACI model clarifies who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. The most important distinction is between responsible and accountable. Many people can be responsible for contributing to work, but only one person should be accountable for the outcome.

A practical example is a customer experience transformation where the organization wants to reduce call handling time and improve first contact resolution. The business may assume operations owns it. Operations may assume technology owns it because the platform needs configuration. Technology may assume training owns it because agent behavior needs to change. Training may assume reporting owns it because success needs to be measured.

The result is delay, rework, and confusion.

A stronger advisory approach is to break the outcome into ownership layers. Who owns the customer journey? Who owns the technical configuration? Who owns the operational readiness? Who owns training adoption? Who owns reporting validation? Who owns post-launch support?

This is also where MECE thinking helps. Instead of allowing overlapping responsibilities, break the work into mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive components. Each workstream should be distinct enough to avoid duplication, but complete enough that nothing important falls through the cracks.

The practical leadership line is simple:

> “I am comfortable with shared input, but we need one clear owner to move this forward.”

Ownership does not reduce collaboration. It protects it. It gives the team a decision path, an escalation path, and a clear way to measure progress.

In transformation, ambiguity is expensive. Clear ownership turns ambition into movement.

**Advisor takeaway:** Shared input is healthy. Shared accountability without a named owner is risky. If everybody owns it, nobody owns it.
