# Discovery Is Risk Reduction

Discovery is often misunderstood as requirements gathering.

That definition is too narrow.

Good discovery is risk reduction.

The goal of discovery is not simply to ask stakeholders what they want. It is to understand the business outcome, current-state friction, operational constraints, decision landscape, technical dependencies, adoption risks, and success measures.

If discovery only captures a wish list, it may produce a solution that is well documented but poorly targeted.

A stronger discovery approach starts with outcomes.

The key questions are:

* What business outcome are we trying to protect or improve?
* What is not working today?
* Who is impacted?
* What happens if we do nothing?
* What constraints exist?
* What assumptions are we making?
* What does success look like?
* What could prevent adoption?

This aligns well with TOGAF’s architecture development discipline. Business architecture should inform application, data, and technology decisions. If the business capability and value stream are unclear, the solution architecture may be technically sound but strategically disconnected.

MECE thinking is also useful during discovery. Requirements should be organized into clear categories to avoid overlap and gaps. For example, a transformation discovery could be structured across customer journey, operations, technology, data and reporting, security, training, change management, governance, and support.

From a Lean Six Sigma perspective, discovery also supports problem definition. The Define phase is critical because a poorly defined problem leads to poor measurement, weak analysis, and ineffective improvement.

Consider a business asking for a new reporting dashboard. A shallow discovery process may ask what fields and charts they want. A stronger discovery process asks what decisions the dashboard needs to support.

That changes everything.

If the decision is workforce planning, the dashboard needs interval-level demand, staffing, adherence, handle time, shrinkage, and forecast accuracy. If the decision is executive performance, the dashboard may need service level, customer satisfaction, cost-to-serve, channel mix, and trend analysis.

The tool request is the same. The business purpose is different.

A useful advisory line is:

> “Before we discuss solutions, I want to understand the business outcome you are trying to protect.”

This positions discovery as a strategic activity rather than an administrative one.

Discovery reduces the risk of solving the wrong problem, designing for the wrong stakeholder, missing a critical dependency, or launching a solution that users do not adopt.

**Advisor takeaway:** Discovery is not a delay before delivery. Discovery is how leaders reduce delivery risk before it becomes expensive.
